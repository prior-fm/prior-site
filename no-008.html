No. 008 · 21 seconds
One more disk doubles the moves

Five disks take 31. A sixth does not take 32 — it takes 63

Three rods, five disks, one rule: never a bigger disk on a smaller one. The whole solution is five lines of recursion — move the four-disk tower out of the way, move the big disk once, move the four-disk tower back on top of it. The film runs all 31 moves at a metronomic tempo while the code panel highlights the recursive call each move belongs to. The big disk moves exactly once, at move 16 — dead centre, and that symmetry is the recursion.

The thing worth noticing
31 is not a choice. It is the minimum.

Showing that your solution takes 2ⁿ − 1 moves proves an upper bound and nothing else. “Optimal” is a claim about all solutions — including strategies nobody has thought of. The pack refuses to recite the formula: a breadth-first search walks every legal position (3ⁿ states — 243 for five disks), with no recursion and no opinion about how the puzzle should be played, and reports the true shortest path from all-on-A to all-on-C.

The two columns agree at every size checked, 1 through 7 disks. The elegant recursion and the exhaustively-verified optimum are the same object — the five lines are not just correct, they are unbeatable. The search takes milliseconds, which is the point: when checking is this cheap, there is no excuse for taking a formula on authority.

And the part the film has no room for
What an exponent feels like

To move n disks you must move n−1 disks twice — off the big disk, then back on top of it — plus one move for the big disk itself. moves(n) = 2·moves(n−1) + 1. That innocent-looking 2 is the whole story: at one move per second, five disks is half a minute, thirty disks is 34 years, and the 64-disk tower of the legend runs 585 billion years — the exponent outlives the sun.

The pack also ships the recursion with the call stack made visible: the same algorithm as a loop over an explicit stack, and a check that the two move lists come out identical rather than a comment saying they do. The transform matters beyond the puzzle — it is how any tree recursion survives when the input goes deeper than Python’s ~1000-frame limit, and the emit-marker trick that preserves the in-order work is the part almost everyone gets wrong first.

The pack
Run it and check the claim

The film’s five lines, the exhaustive minimality search, and the doubling table. Python, no dependencies.
