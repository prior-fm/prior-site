The four signals

The film’s checkbox “checks four things”: cookie, browser, network, movement. Three of those are states a real CAPTCHA reads from your session — whether it has seen this cookie before, whether the browser looks like a browser, whether the network address has a history — and the film simply declares them: pass or fail. The fourth is the one a cursor path makes measurable, and it is the one this page computes.

Movement is four numbers read off the path, sampled at a fixed rate:

wander1 − chord ÷ path length. A dead-straight line scores 0.× 8
tempostandard deviation of speed ÷ mean speed. Constant speed scores 0.× 4
turnmean change of heading between moving samples, in radians. No turning scores 0.× 3
pausesthe share of samples that moved less than 2 px. Weighted against you.× −4

One logistic unit turns those into a probability: z = 8·wander + 4·tempo + 3·turn − 4·pauses − 2.3, movement = 1 / (1 + e−z). The score is the mean of the four signals, clamped to 0.1–0.9 and shown to one decimal, which is how reCAPTCHA v3 scores look in practice.

Try the box with the mouse, then with a finger. Then press Straight line.

Why a careful human scores 0.4

The film’s third visitor moves like everyone else. Its movement comes out at 0.73 — wander 0.119, tempo 0.584, turn 0.075, pauses 0.051 — inside the range of the other twenty-one humans, whose lowest is 0.56. On movement alone it passes.

What sinks it is the two declared signals the film paints amber: cookie fail, network fail. Mean of 0.73, 0, 1, 0 is 0.43. Shown to one decimal: 0.4. Nothing about the hand was wrong. The model is not deciding whether you are human; it is scoring whether your session looks like the sessions it has seen. A fresh browser on a new network, first visit, nothing stored — nothing here knows them.

You can watch it happen above: draw a path that scores 0.9, then switch cookie and network to fail. Same hand, 0.4.

The puzzle is not the test

The picture puzzle is what happens after the score, when it is too low to wave through and too high to block. It is not measuring you; it is buying time and a second signal. By the time you see it the decision has been made and you are being asked to appeal it.

What a low score looks like: the film’s bot arrives in a dead-straight line at constant speed. Wander 0.0002, tempo 0.035, turn 0.028, pauses 0. Movement 0.11. Every declared signal fails. Mean 0.03, clamped up to the floor: 0.1. That is the path that gets the traffic lights.

And the other way round: press Straight line with all three declared signals passing. Movement is 0.09, and the score is still 0.8. Movement alone cannot fail you. Movement alone cannot save you either. Three of the four numbers were never about the path.

What this page does not do

It is not reCAPTCHA. Nobody outside Google knows what reCAPTCHA weighs, and this page does not claim to. It is the film’s stated model — four signals, one logistic unit, a mean, a clamp — and the film’s numbers are what it prints.

The three declared signals are toggles because they cannot be measured in a page. A cookie history, a browser fingerprint, a network reputation are things a server accumulates across many visits; a single self-contained HTML file has none of that and would be lying if it pretended to. So you set them, and the page shows you what they do to the number.

The model was tuned on three paths and held against all twenty-three the film draws. The gate, from the build’s own data file: bot movement under 0.20 — true (0.1117); every human movement over 0.50 — true, lowest 0.5634; bot score under 0.20 — true; the careful human under 0.50 on score and passing on movement — true and true; the three shown scores distinct — true. If that gate had failed, the film would not have been built.

Nothing leaves the browser. The path you draw is sampled, scored and drawn here and is gone when you close the tab. There is no request in this file except the site’s page counter, which sees a page view and nothing else.

What this page is actually doing

Sampling on a clock, not on events. Browsers fire pointer events at whatever rate the hardware produces them, which would make speed and pauses depend on your mouse. So the box reads the pointer’s last position every 33 ms on a fixed timer — the same cadence as the film’s 30 frames a second — and each dot on the trail is one sample. A pause is a stack of dots.

One scale. The film’s paths are in its source frame, 720 px wide. The box maps its own width to 720 px before scoring, so the 2 px pause threshold means the same thing on a phone as on a monitor, and your features sit beside the film’s on the same axis.

One function. The scorer is written once. Your path, the straight line and the three film paths all go through it. On load the page runs the film’s three through that function and compares the result with the numbers the film shows — 0.9, 0.1, 0.4, and each movement to four decimals — and with the straight line’s 0.8. If any of them disagree the line under the stats turns red and says so, rather than quietly showing you one of them.
