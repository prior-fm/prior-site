The optimal answer is not about who moves fastest

Four people cross a bridge at night with one torch. Two at a time, at the slower one’s pace, and someone has to carry the torch back. Every schedule in the film is replayed move by move against the rules, and both totals on screen come out of that replay rather than being typed in.

The puzzle
1, 2, 5
and 10.

Four people, one torch, one bridge. Two can cross at a time, and a crossing takes the slower person’s time. The torch cannot be thrown back, so somebody has to walk it. Their times are 1, 2, 5 and 10 minutes. What is the fastest everyone gets across?

Almost everyone lands on the same idea: put the fastest person in charge. Send 1 with each of the others in turn and let them run the torch back every time. 1+10, 1 back, 1+5, 1 back, 1+2. It is tidy, it is obviously sensible, and it costs 19 minutes.

The thing worth noticing
Send the
two slowest
together.

A crossing costs the slower person’s time. So a slow person is only expensive when they are the slowest one moving.

Escorting pays the 10 once and the 5 once — fifteen minutes of slow — and buys cheap one-minute returns in exchange. Sending 5 and 10 together pays the 10 once and the 5 not at all, at the cost of one two-minute return instead of a one-minute one. That trade is worth two minutes:

1+2 across (2) · 1 back (1) · 5+10 across (10) · 2 back (2) · 1+2 across (2). 17 minutes.

The two fastest are not the workhorses here. They are the shuttle. You give up the neat structure for one move so the bottleneck can be paid for once instead of twice.

How narrow the optimum is
2 out of
85,368.

The pack enumerates every legal schedule for these four people: 85,368 of them, up to a cost of 40 minutes. Exactly two cost 17. Twelve cost 19. The distribution peaks around 37.

So the intuitive answer is not a bad answer — it is in the best 0.03% of everything you could do. It is just not the best, and the gap is entirely about how many times you pay for the slowest person.

The pack — free, no email
The solver,
and the
proof

It does not assert that 17 is optimal. It searches for it, then checks itself against a second, independent derivation.

bridge.py — Python 3.8+, standard library only, no installs and no arguments needed. Four things, and they are deliberately separate so no one of them can quietly be the others:

1. A replayer that refuses. Every schedule is walked move by move, and each move is checked before its cost is added: one or two people, always on the torch’s side, always the slower one’s time, torch ending on the far bank. A schedule that breaks a rule raises rather than scoring.

2. Exhaustive search. Dijkstra over the full state graph — every subset of people on each bank, times two torch positions. Not a heuristic and not a greedy rule: it searches everything reachable.

3. An independent cross-check. The classic closed form, implemented separately from the search. If the two ever disagree the script exits non-zero. This is the part that matters: a search bug and a formula bug are very unlikely to agree, and a single implementation that is wrong is perfectly happy to look right.

4. The full count. Every legal schedule, with its cost distribution printed as a histogram, so “optimal” is a claim you can see the shape of.

Run it on your own numbers — python bridge.py 1 2 5 10 15 20 — and watch the shuttle strategy take over as the group gets slower.

Where this actually turns up

The puzzle is a toy, and the shape of it is not. Any pipeline where a step costs the slowest item in the batch has this structure: batched inference, CI jobs fanned across runners, a straggler in a distributed reduce, a nightly job whose runtime is the longest partition. The instinct is always to make the fast path faster. The lever is almost always how often the slow thing gets triggered — group the expensive work so it is paid for once, and accept a slightly worse cheap move to get it.

Which is the film’s last line: it is not about who moves fastest. It is about minimising how often the slowest constraint gets triggered.
