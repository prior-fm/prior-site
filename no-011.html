No. 011 · 20 seconds
0.1° apart. Then 190°.

Fifty double pendulums released a tenth of a degree apart, and twenty seconds of real physics

Fifty double pendulums, each swinging in its own vertical plane, the fifty planes fanned evenly around one shared pivot. Their release angles differ by exactly a tenth of a degree — the code asserts that to machine precision before it integrates anything. For about ten seconds they move as one and the colours read as a smooth spectrum. Then the ordering comes apart, and by the end adjacent hues are nowhere near each other. Nothing is faked: it is a fixed-step RK4 integration that conserves energy to 1.3×10⁻¹¹ of the energy scale over the full twenty seconds.

The thing worth noticing
Chaos is not randomness.

There is no random number generator anywhere in this simulation. Run it twice and you get the same twenty seconds, to the last decimal place. The whole film is decided by one line of code — the one that sets fifty starting angles a tenth of a degree apart.

What makes it chaotic is not disorder but sensitivity: the system pulls nearby starting points apart exponentially. At this release angle it does so by a factor of e roughly every second. A tenth of a degree survives that for about ten seconds, and then it doesn’t. Turn the energy down — run the pack with --theta0 40 — and the same fifty pendulums stay in step for the entire run. The chaos is not in the equations. It is in how hard you push them.

This is why a weather forecast dies at about two weeks, why a physics simulation won’t reproduce across two machines with different floating-point behaviour, and why “we changed the seed and got a different answer” is sometimes the system telling you the truth rather than a bug.

The number that should change how you plan
18,000× for ten more seconds.

Nearby states separate like δ(t) = δ₀·e^(λt), so the time before your initial error swamps the answer is t = (1/λ)·ln(Δ/δ₀). Read that carefully: your prediction horizon grows only with the LOGARITHM of your precision.

For this film’s pendulums λ is 0.98 per second, so the fifty hold together about ten seconds. Want twenty? You need e¹⁰ᵧ ≈ 18,000× more precision. Then another 18,000× for the ten after that. Halving your error buys you 0.7 seconds — always, no matter how small the error already is.

That is why chaos is not a measurement problem you can spend your way out of, and why the two-week ceiling on weather forecasting is a property of the atmosphere rather than of anyone’s compute budget.

The papers, a primer, and the code

Seven foundational papers in reading order, the long explanation, and the exact simulation from the film.

PAPERS.md — Lorenz 1963 (the one that started it), Shinbrot et al. 1992 (a real double pendulum on a bench, with the divergence measured), Li & Yorke 1975 (the paper that named chaos), May 1976, Feigenbaum 1978, Wolf et al. 1985 (how you estimate a Lyapunov exponent from data you did not generate), and Zhang et al. 2019 on the two-week limit. Each one gets what it established and why it matters if you build models. Every citation was checked against the publisher’s own record, not quoted from memory.

PRIMER.md — why deterministic and unpredictable are not opposites, the one equation worth carrying, and the four places this shows up in real work: reproducibility across hardware, forecast horizons, seed sensitivity, and measuring λ instead of arguing about it.

chaos.py — standard library only, no numpy, nothing to install. It integrates at a five-times coarser timestep than the film and lands on the same numbers to four significant figures. It also ships the check that saved this film: a double pendulum conserves energy exactly, and a wrong set of equations does not — while still producing a smooth, convincing, entirely fictional animation. That happened here. The first formulation was stable, returned the identical trajectory at three different timesteps, and lost 0.169 joules a second where the correct one loses 2.3×10⁻¹³. Nothing but the energy gate would have caught it.
