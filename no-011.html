Fifty double pendulums released a tenth of a degree apart, and twenty seconds of real physics

Fifty double pendulums, each swinging in its own vertical plane, the fifty planes fanned evenly around one shared pivot. Their release angles differ by exactly a tenth of a degree — the code asserts that to machine precision before it integrates anything. For about ten seconds they move as one and the colours read as a smooth spectrum. Then the ordering comes apart, and by the end adjacent hues are nowhere near each other. Nothing is faked: it is a fixed-step RK4 integration that conserves energy to 1.3×10⁻¹¹ of the energy scale over the full twenty seconds.

The thing worth noticing
Chaos is
not
randomness.

There is no random number generator anywhere in this simulation. Run it twice and you get the same twenty seconds, to the last decimal place. The whole film is decided by one line of code — the one that sets fifty starting angles a tenth of a degree apart.

What makes it chaotic is not disorder but sensitivity: the system pulls nearby starting points apart exponentially. At this release angle it does so by a factor of e roughly every second. A tenth of a degree survives that for about ten seconds, and then it doesn’t. Turn the energy down — run the pack with --theta0 40 — and the same fifty pendulums stay in step for the entire run. The chaos is not in the equations. It is in how hard you push them.

This is why a weather forecast dies at about two weeks, why a physics simulation won’t reproduce across two machines with different floating-point behaviour, and why “we changed the seed and got a different answer” is sometimes the system telling you the truth rather than a bug.

The pack
Run the
whole thing
yourself

One Python file, standard library only. No numpy, no plotting library, nothing to install.

python chaos.py re-runs the exact simulation in the film and prints the divergence schedule; --svg out.svg draws the trails. It integrates at a five-times coarser timestep than the film and lands on the same numbers to four significant figures, which is what a converged integration is supposed to do.

It also ships the check that saved the film. A double pendulum conserves energy exactly — and a wrong set of equations does not, while still producing a smooth, convincing, entirely fictional animation. That happened here: the first formulation was stable, returned the identical trajectory at three different timesteps, and lost 0.169 joules a second where the correct one loses 2.3×10⁻¹³. The energy gate is the only thing that caught it. The README tells that story properly.
