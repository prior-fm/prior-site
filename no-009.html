Dijkstra checks 38,750 corners of Madrid. Bidirectional A* finds the identical route checking 3,033

Five algorithms, one real map, one query. Every search in the film is a real instrumented run on the real Madrid street network — 72,234 corners of it, downloaded from OpenStreetMap — and every Nodes/Time caption is that run’s own measurement. Dijkstra floods half the city. Greedy best-first sprints straight at the goal and touches 622 corners. A* and its bidirectional variant get the best of both: the optimal route, a fraction of the work. Nothing is animated by hand.

The thing worth noticing
Greedy is
fast. And
greedy is
wrong.

The fastest search in the race — 622 corners, about 2 milliseconds — hands back a route 23.6% longer than the optimum. Greedy best-first ranks its frontier only by the distance still to go, so it never reconsiders a step that looked good at the time. That is not a bug in the implementation; it is the definition of the algorithm, and the race measures its price to the metre: 10.35 km against the true 8.37.

A* makes one change: rank by distance travelled plus the guess of what is left. That single addition restores the optimality guarantee — and the pack refuses to take that on authority. It asserts, in code, on every run, that Dijkstra, A* and both bidirectional variants return routes of identical length. If any of them ever disagreed, the script fails loudly instead of drawing a pretty picture.

And the part the film has no room for
What a
heuristic
buys you

A heuristic is a guess at the distance left — here, the straight-line distance to the goal. It cannot overestimate, and that one property is the whole contract: it lets A* ignore almost the entire map while still promising the best answer. Watch the shapes in the film: Dijkstra’s circle knows nothing, so it grows evenly in every direction. A*’s ellipse leans at the goal. Bidirectional A* is two thin corridors that meet in the middle — 3,033 corners touched out of 72,234, 92% less work than Dijkstra for the same route.

This is the trade that runs your maps app, your game NPCs, and half of classical AI planning: pay a tiny cost per node to think about where the goal is, and stop paying the enormous cost of looking everywhere. The race makes the trade visible — and because the runs are real, the numbers move honestly when you change the city.

The pack
Race them
on your
own city

All five algorithms, the real street network, the optimality assertion. Python, no dependencies.

python race.py and nothing else. It downloads central Madrid’s drivable street network from OpenStreetMap (once, then cached), builds the 72,234-corner graph, races all five algorithms on the film’s exact query, and prints the table — corners touched, wall time, route length — with the optimality check asserted in code. It also writes an SVG per algorithm so you can see precisely what each one explored. --city berlin or --bbox S W N E races anywhere on Earth.
